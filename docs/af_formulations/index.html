Same benefit, different label

How the choice of avoided-loss formulation moves a practice's yield benefit between the production and adaptation labels, and what that means for carbon-finance co-benefit claims. Maize in Kenya, Ethiopia and Nigeria, on Atlas hazard regimes.

Exploratory. Every number is illustrative. Loss shares are bronze placeholders, buffering is a scenario grid, practice gains are ERA medians pooled across seasons. Nothing here is an estimate for reporting. See Limits.

The one-line result

Defining "no adaptation" as same tonnes lost (levels difference-in-differences, which is how the CGIAR MELIAF indicator v3.0 defines its factor) rather than same share lost (proportional split) moves roughly a fifth to a third of a yield-raising practice's benefit from the adaptation label to the production label. The total does not change. The current CC4A pipeline sits between the two and over-counts the total.

The size of that shift has a closed form: per stress year the gap between the two readings is −L·B·g, so its expected annual value is −g · B · E[L]. It does not depend on how much the practice buffers. Relative to the proportional split it is −g / ((1+g)·m), the same in every place for a given buffering. Location only scales the absolute size, through baseline yield and the hazard regime.

The stress-year gap between practice and control decomposes into four terms:

As − Bs  =  (A − B)  −  L·(A − B)  +  B·L·m  +  g·B·L·m

normal gain · hazard bite on the gain · loss avoided on the baseline · loss avoided on the extra yield. The formulations differ only in where they file the second and fourth terms.

Why severity classes stay

When buffering weakens with severity (0.75 moderate, 0.50 severe, 0.25 extreme), the effective buffering a unit experiences falls as extremes take a larger share of its expected loss. A single stress-or-normal factor would hide this; severity-resolved factors transfer across places.

What the ERA season split shows

MELIAF needs gnorm and gstress estimated separately. ERA maize trials for the two practices were classified season by season from observed daily climate (CHIRPS v3 rainfall, AgERA5 temperature) on each site's own growing-season window, with the Atlas severity bands. Three things came out.

1. The climate-specific increment is small and positive

Study-averaged mean log response ratio, back-transformed. A site-season is "stress" when its NDWS band is at or above the threshold. Heat stress (NTx35) almost never occurs at ERA maize sites, so there is no heat estimate.

2. Stress-season effects scale with yield: proportional, not additive

On stress seasons only. Proportional regresses log(MeanT/MeanC) on log control yield; additive regresses MeanT − MeanC on control yield. t-statistics with study-clustered standard errors. A slope near zero is the model the data support: in three of four cases the ratio is flat and the difference is not, which is the proportional split (F3). The two tests are not symmetric and power is low.

3. Observed stress frequency at ERA sites is far below the Atlas P

Share of ERA site-seasons at or above the band (observed, 1981–2016, site windows) against the Atlas P(h, ≥ moderate) of the admin-2 unit containing each site (NEX-GDDP 1995–2014, maize jagermeyr window). The observed share sits near MELIAF's ~0.2 benchmarks; the Atlas value is three to four times higher. Candidates: the Atlas's single maize window (for Kenyan pixels the short rains), GCM-based frequencies, water-balance parameters, and ERA sites being better-watered than their units. A rerun on the Atlas window at the same sites is the first test.

Three things this comparison cannot settle

  • It is a labelling sensitivity, not a test. Stress-year yields are constructed as As = A(1 − L(1−m)), which bakes in "same share lost". A fair test needs empirical stress-season yields: classify ERA site-seasons with the Atlas hazard stack and ask whether the stress-season effect scales with control yield (proportional) or not (additive).
  • MELIAF v3.0 is levels DiD by definition. It defines g in kg/ha. Reading the factor in relative terms is a change request, not a clarification.
  • Benchmarks differ. Atlas "≥ moderate" gives Pstress of 0.5–0.85; MELIAF's candidate benchmarks sit near 0.2. The severity-resolved factor merges exactly to a single factor, but not across benchmarks.

What the CCA analysis does

  • Use the proportional split (F3): production in a stress year = (A − B)(1 − L); avoided loss = A·L·m. Keep coefficients signed; no floors.
  • Correct the pipeline: step 2 production scaled by (1 − Σ P·L) in stress years; step 3 avoided loss on the practice yield A, not the control B; remove the [0, 1] clamp on m.
  • Keep severity classes as the primitive and serve MELIAF one factor per hazard by the exact merge.
  • Report per hazard until co-occurrence is handled; P(no hazard) and P(dry ∩ wet) are already in the outputs.

Questions for Adaptation Insights

  1. When gstress and gnorm are estimated, will they be ratios or kg/ha? If kg/ha, which yield converts them? (The v3.0 reference database is a design, not yet a dataset, so this is a choice still open.)
  2. Can the control's stress-season loss Lc = 1 − Bs/B be recorded alongside each factor? Without it neither reading converts to the other.
  3. Which hazard benchmark, and would a severity-resolved factor with the exact merge be accepted as the primitive?
  4. Shared task: classify ERA maize site-seasons as stress or normal, so gnorm and gstress can be estimated separately for the practices in scope. The CGIAR climate-toolkit already does this at site level: NDWS, NDWL0 and NTx35 from observed daily CHIRPS v3 and AgERA5, Atlas-style severity bands, a sites table in and a season classification out. Its heat bands (10 / 20 / 25 days) would be set to the Atlas values (7 / 14 / 21) for the run.

Same benefit, different label

A farming practice such as cover crops can raise yields in a good year and protect yields in a bad one. Carbon-finance schemes pay an extra premium only for the second part, the adaptation benefit. This page shows that how much of a practice's benefit counts as "adaptation" depends on a choice of definition, how big that choice is on real African hazard data, and what we learned when we tested it against field trials.

Exploratory. Every number is illustrative. Some inputs are placeholders, some are scenarios, and the trial evidence is thin. Nothing here is an estimate for reporting. See What to trust.

    The words used on this page

    TermMeaning here
    PracticeThe farming change being credited: cover crops, or reduced tillage. Always compared with a control: the same farm without the practice.
    Normal year, stress yearA season without a damaging climate hazard, and one with (for example a dry spell).
    Yield gain (g)How much more the practice yields than the control in a normal year, as a percentage. +16 % means the practice yields 16 % more.
    The share of its yield the control loses in a stress year. 50 % means a bad year halves the control's yield.
    Buffering (m)The share of that loss the practice avoids. 0.5 means the practice halves the damage; 0 means it does nothing in a bad year; a negative value means it makes the bad year worse.
    Production benefitThe part of the practice's benefit that would exist even in a climate with no shocks.
    Adaptation benefit (avoided loss)The extra benefit that appears only under stress. This is the part claimed as a climate co-benefit.
    Hazard frequencyHow often stress seasons occur at a place, as a share of seasons. The Atlas (Africa Agriculture Adaptation Atlas) publishes this for three severity bands: moderate, severe, extreme.
    ERAEvidence for Resilient Agriculture: a database of African field trials comparing practices with controls, site by site and season by season.
    MELIAF indicatorCGIAR's draft adaptation indicator. It measures a practice's effect in stress seasons minus its effect in normal seasons, times how often stress seasons occur. We aim to be compatible with it, not to adopt it unchanged.
    DistrictSecond-level administrative unit (county sub-unit in Kenya, LGA in Nigeria, woreda in Ethiopia). The smallest area the Atlas publishes hazard frequency for.

    The result in one paragraph

    There are two reasonable ways to define what "no adaptation" means. One says a practice adapts only if it loses fewer tonnes than the control in a bad year. The other says it adapts if it loses a smaller share of its own yield. For a practice that raises yields, the first definition moves roughly a fifth to a third of its benefit out of the adaptation column and into the production column. The total benefit does not change; only the label does. CGIAR's draft indicator uses the first definition. Our current analysis pipeline sits between the two and also over-counts the total.

    How big the shift is follows a simple rule. Per stress year it equals the loss share × the baseline yield × the yield gain. So it is largest where yields are high, gains are large and stress is frequent, and it does not depend on how well the practice buffers. As a share of the adaptation benefit it depends only on the yield gain and the buffering, the same in every place.

    What this page contains

    Part 1 generalises the question across the map. It takes every maize-growing district in Kenya, Ethiopia and Nigeria, with the Atlas's hazard frequencies and MapSPAM yields, and asks how much the labelling rule matters there. Its loss shares are placeholders and its buffering is a scenario grid, so it answers "how big could the choice be, and where", not "what is the adaptation benefit".

    Part 2 goes back to field trials. It takes the ERA trials behind the two practices, works out season by season whether each trial sat in a stress season, and measures the practices' effects in stress and normal seasons directly. That gives the first real evidence on which rule fits the data, and a check on the Atlas frequencies Part 1 relies on. Section 3 documents how Part 2 was done; Section 4 says what to trust; Sections 5 and 6 give the recommendation and the open questions.

    Part 1 · The labelling choice, generalised across districts

    Inputs: Atlas hazard frequencies by district (1995–2014, three severity bands), MapSPAM 2020 maize yields, ERA yield gains, a placeholder loss-share table and ten buffering scenarios. Every district is run through all six rules. Nothing in Part 1 is an estimate; it is a sensitivity test of the labelling rule.

    1.1 Six rules for one benefit

    Six labelling rules were compared. They all start from the same facts: the control's yield in a normal year (B), the practice's yield in a normal year (A, which is B plus the gain), the loss share (L) and the buffering (m). Three of them split the stress-year benefit without losing or inventing anything; three do not.

    Part 2 · What the field trials show

    Part 1 uses placeholder loss shares, scenario buffering and a yield gain pooled over all seasons. Part 2 goes to the evidence: the ERA maize trials for both practices (1,245 comparisons, 106 studies, 111 sites). For every trial season we worked out from rainfall and temperature records whether it was a stress season or a normal one, then measured the practice's effect in each kind of season. This is site-level evidence, not a map; it feeds Part 1's yield gain and tests Part 1's assumptions. Five things came out.

    2.1 The practices do better in stress seasons than in normal ones, on few studies

    Table 1. Practice effect on maize yield in normal and stress seasons, by hazard and stress threshold.

    Average effect across studies in normal seasons and in stress seasons, as a percentage change in yield, rainfed trials only. "Moderate" and "severe" are the stress thresholds used. Cover crops under dry stress rest on about a dozen studies at the moderate threshold and a handful at the severe one. Heat stress almost never occurred at these maize sites on site windows, so there is no heat estimate.

    2.2 Does the stress-season effect scale with yield? The trials do not settle it

    Table 2. Scaling test on stress seasons: does the effect depend on the control's yield level?

    Two regressions on stress seasons only, rainfed trials, yields in Mg/ha. If the practice protects a share of yield, its effect as a ratio should not depend on how high the control's yield is; if it protects a fixed number of tonnes, the difference in tonnes should not. The t-statistic says how far each slope is from zero (below 2 is not distinguishable from zero). The four cases split: reduced tillage under dry stress behaves like a share, cover crops under waterlogging like fixed tonnes, cover crops under dry stress is ambiguous, and for reduced tillage under waterlogging both slopes are non-zero, so neither simple model fits. An earlier version of this table mixed kg/ha and Mg/ha rows and wrongly favoured "same share"; this is the corrected result. The test is weak; the two regressions are not symmetric and the samples are small.

    2.3 Stress seasons are rarer in the trial records than the Atlas says

    Table 3. Share of trial seasons classed as stress seasons, from weather records on two season definitions, against the Atlas frequency for the containing district.

    Share of trial seasons that were stress seasons according to the rainfall and temperature records, against the Atlas's hazard frequency for the district containing each site. "Site window" scores each site on its own growing season (trial dates where recorded, otherwise the local rainfall pattern); "Atlas-style window" rescores the same sites on the standard maize calendar the Atlas uses, still from weather records. In Kenya the trial record stays near one stress season in five on either window, while the Atlas's district frequency says two in three.

    2.4 The definition of the growing season matters a lot

    Table 4. The same trial seasons scored on the site window and on the standard maize calendar, by country.

    Same trial seasons scored both ways. Where the standard maize calendar runs 190–225 days (southern Africa) it takes in dry months before and after the real season and doubles the count of stress seasons; northern Nigeria behaves the same. Where it is the 85–95-day short rains (Kenya) it changes little. The normal-versus-stress comparison in point 1 also changes with the window, so the season definition is part of any adaptation factor, not a detail.

    3 · How Part 2 was done

    Short version here; the full methods, with references and a step-by-step code trace, are in methods.md in the same folder of the repository. Every subsection names the script that implements it.

    3.1 Tools used

    The season-by-season stress classification was done with the CGIAR climate-toolkit (commit 453c12a, July 2026), a Python package from the CGIAR Climate Data Hub. For each trial site and season it fetched the daily climate records through Google Earth Engine, ran its soil-water balance, and returned the counts of water-stress, waterlogging and heat days for the season window it was given. Its built-in GGCMI crop-calendar lookup supplied the "standard maize calendar" windows. Its chunk cache made re-runs cheap.

    Done outside the toolkit, in this repository: choosing each site's season window (trial dates, else the local rainfall pattern); converting the toolkit's season totals into the Atlas's monthly-mean classes and thresholds (the toolkit's own heat thresholds differ from the Atlas's); matching trial seasons to ERA comparisons; the effect split and regressions; and the join to the Atlas district values. Two workarounds were needed and are flagged to the toolkit maintainers: the hazard function does not accept a climate model and scenario, so the NEX-GDDP runs set the source module's defaults; and a season window that crosses 31 December needs climate data fetched into the following year.

    The Atlas's own season classification was not used and is not public. The Atlas publishes 20-year frequencies by district, not which seasons were dry. Every "stress season" on this page was computed here, Atlas-style (same indices, same thresholds), from weather records or climate-model output. The Atlas enters only as the district frequency it is compared against.

    3.2 Trial data

    Script 03b_era_site_years.R. From the ERA database we took every maize yield comparison for the two practices: ERA's "Green Manure" entries for cover crops and its "Reduced Tillage" entries (no-till and minimum tillage) for reduced tillage. Only comparisons where the practice is the single difference between the two arms were kept; bundles such as mulch plus reduced tillage on one side were excluded. Management shared by both arms (fertiliser, varieties) stays and varies between studies. Comparisons ERA flags as invalid, or with a missing or zero yield on either side, were dropped; yields in kg/ha were converted to Mg/ha; 32 comparisons from irrigated trials were set aside. That leaves 1,245 comparisons from 106 studies at 111 sites, 1971–2016; 287 of the 327 site-years fall in the period with daily rainfall records (1981 onwards).

    Each comparison carries the trial's coordinates with a precision radius (median 0.9 km; 81 % of sites within 5 km, a few at country scale), the measurement year, and for bimodal sites which rainy season it was (ERA marks these as season 1 or 2; where unmarked, season 1 is assumed). Both planting and harvest dates are recorded for about a quarter of site-years.

    3.3 Defining each trial's growing season

    Script 03c_era_site_windows.R. Stress is only meaningful inside the growing season, so every site needed a season window. Three sources, in order of preference:

    1. ERA planting and harvest dates where the trial recorded them (33 of 128 site-seasons). For a site with several dated years the most common planting and harvest day-of-year was used.
    2. The local rainfall pattern otherwise (95 site-seasons). From the WorldClim monthly rainfall climatology at the site, a month is "wet" if it averages 60 mm or more; a run of two or more wet months is a rainy season; the window runs from the first wet month to one month after the run ends, capped at five months, so a three-month rainy season gives a four-month maize season; a site wet all year gets its wettest five months. Where the climatology shows two rainy seasons, ERA's season 1 is matched to the earlier run and season 2 to the later one. Thirteen site-seasons are marked season 2 at sites whose climatology shows only one rainy season; they were scored on that single season and flagged.
    3. The standard maize calendar (GGCMI, the one the Atlas uses) as a last resort, and as the deliberate alternative in the "Atlas-style window" runs. That calendar has one season per place, so in those runs the second-season trials at bimodal sites (193 comparisons) have no matching season and drop out; Tables 3 and 4 and the cover-crop reversal in 2.4 carry that caveat.

    3.4 Climate records and the water balance

    Script 07_era_seasons.py. Daily rainfall came from CHIRPS v3 (satellite plus gauges, about 5 km grid) and daily maximum and minimum temperature from AgERA5 (about 10 km grid), both read through Google Earth Engine for the pixel containing each site. The CGIAR climate-toolkit then ran a daily soil-water balance for each season: reference evapotranspiration by the Hargreaves method from the temperatures; maize crop coefficients 0.3, 1.2 and 0.6 for early, mid and late season (its FAO-56 starter values); rooting depth from the HWSD soil map capped at 1.5 m; plant-available water capacity from SoilGrids field capacity and wilting point over that depth (limited to 25–400 mm) and a saturation store from SoilGrids porosity (limited to 20–300 mm); a 60-day spin-up before the window. The values used at each site are written to every output row. From that balance it counted, within the window, the days when crop water use fell below half of demand (water-stress days, the Atlas's "NDWS"), the days with soil water above field capacity (waterlogging days, "NDWL0"), and the days with maximum temperature at or above 35 °C (heat days, "NTx35").

    3.5 From day counts to stress classes

    Script 07_era_seasons.py, constant BANDS. The Atlas classes a season by the average monthly count of such days over the season months, against fixed thresholds: water stress 15 / 20 / 25 days a month for moderate / severe / extreme, waterlogging 2 / 5 / 8, heat 7 / 14 / 21. To use the same classes, each season's day count was divided by the site's mean window length in months (count × 365.25/12 ÷ mean days in the window) and compared with the same thresholds. A trial season is then a "stress season" for a hazard if its class is at or above the chosen threshold (moderate by default, severe as a sensitivity). A season can be a stress season for more than one hazard.

    3.6 Splitting the practice effect by season type

    Script 08_era_split.R, rainfed comparisons only. Each comparison's effect is the log of practice yield over control yield. Within each study the effects were averaged separately over its stress seasons and its normal seasons, then averaged across studies with equal weight, and converted back to a percentage. The stress minus normal difference is the climate-specific effect the CGIAR indicator asks for. The scaling test used stress-season comparisons at the moderate threshold: a regression of the log ratio on the log of control yield (if the practice protects a share of yield, this slope should be near zero), and a regression of the yield difference in Mg/ha on control yield (if it protects a fixed number of tonnes, this one should). Standard errors allow for comparisons from the same study being correlated. Minimum 10 comparisons from 3 studies.

    3.7 Comparing with the Atlas and with climate models

    Scripts 09_obs_vs_atlas.R, 10_gcm_vs_obs.py, 11_gcm_summary.R. For the Kenyan sites that fall inside maize-growing districts, the same window and the same toolkit water balance were run three more ways: on the weather records for every season from 1995 to 2013 (not only trial years), and on daily output from two of the Atlas's eighteen climate models (ACCESS-CM2 and GFDL-ESM4, NEX-GDDP historical runs) for the same seasons. The Atlas value for comparison is the district's published hazard frequency, recovered in 05_compute.R as the share of maize production in the "dry" classes over all production, using the district's own row where it is complete and backed by at least 100 t of maize and otherwise the province's row.

    3.8 Assumptions

    • A trial's climate is the climate of the 5–10 km pixel containing its coordinates; on-farm and on-station plots at one site share a season.
    • The growing season is the same every year at a site (from dates, climatology or calendar); no year-specific onset.
    • The soil-water balance with the toolkit's FAO-56 starter crop parameters and SoilGrids/HWSD soil and root-depth values represents the trial plots' water status.
    • Scaling a season total of stress days to a monthly mean reproduces the Atlas's seasonal classification.
    • The effect of the practice in a season is independent of other seasons (no carry-over), and comparisons within a study can be averaged.
    • ERA's practice labels map cleanly: "Green Manure" is the cover-crop practice; "Reduced Tillage" is the reduced-tillage practice without residue or mulch bundles.
    • The Atlas district frequency is comparable with a point frequency because it is production-weighted within the district.

    3.9 Sources of error

    • Season window. The largest known one: scoring the same seasons on the standard maize calendar instead of the site window raises the share of stress seasons by factors between 1.1 and 4.7 depending on country, and changes the stress-versus-normal split (partly because second-season trials drop out). Three-quarters of site-years have neither planting nor harvest date recorded.
    • Bimodal sites. Which rainy season a trial used is known only from ERA's season flag, missing for over half the comparisons; thirteen "season 2" site-seasons have no second rainy season in the climatology.
    • Location precision. Coordinates carry a radius from 12 m to 500 km (median 0.9 km; 14 sites beyond 50 km); a 5 km rainfall pixel and a 10 km temperature pixel can straddle a slope or a valley.
    • Rainfall and temperature records. CHIRPS and AgERA5 are gridded estimates; gauge density in the East African highlands is low and convective rain is patchy, so a site's actual rainfall can differ from its pixel.
    • Water-balance parameters. Crop coefficients are the toolkit's generic starter values and soil and root-depth values come from global maps; a different choice shifts every season's day count and so the class boundaries. This is also the leading candidate for the Atlas-side part of the Kenya gap.
    • Partial months. Dividing by window length in months is not identical to the Atlas's mean of calendar-month counts when a window starts or ends mid-month.
    • Small samples. Cover crops under dry stress rest on ten studies at the moderate threshold and two at the severe one; heat stress almost never occurred.
    • Trial management. Controls range from unfertilised to fertilised and improved varieties; on-station trials may be irrigated or protected in ways the climate record cannot see; trial years may avoid failed seasons (the Kenya check found no such bias, but it was one country).
    • Study weighting. Studies count equally regardless of size or precision; no publication-bias correction.
    • Climate models. Two of eighteen Atlas members; the ensemble mean could sit higher or lower.
    • Data access. Two of 237 site-slot fetches failed on transient network errors and were re-run; the toolkit's local cache was reused across runs and is not versioned.

    3.10 What was not done

    • No year-specific onset detection; no use of the toolkit's automatic season detection, which fails on humid sites.
    • No calibration of the water balance against observed soil moisture or crop stress at any site.
    • No heat-stress estimate (too few heat seasons), no sorghum, wheat or bean, and no countries beyond the three for the Atlas comparison.
    • The control's own stress-season loss was not estimated: ERA has no same-site normal-year control to compare with, so the "loss share" remains a placeholder throughout.

    4 · What to trust

    InputStatus
    Hazard frequenciesReal, with caveats. Atlas values for 1995–2014 from an ensemble of climate models, by district, for the standard maize season only. About a third of districts use their province's value because the Atlas grid cannot resolve them. In about one district in sixteen stress occurs in nearly every season, so there is no normal year to compare against. Irrigated districts are treated as rainfed.
    Baseline yieldsReal. MapSPAM 2020 district yields, adjusted upward to a normal-year yield using the expected loss.
    Loss sharesPlaceholder. Expert guesses for maize (10 / 25 / 45 % for moderate / severe / extreme dry stress; 5 / 15 / 30 % for waterlogging), the same everywhere.
    BufferingScenario. No evidence-based values exist yet.
    Yield gainsReal, two versions. From ERA trials: cover crops about +16 % (31 studies), reduced tillage about −3 % (79 studies). The pooled version mixes normal and stress seasons; the normal-season version from point 1 above is also available. The two are close.
    PricesNot applied. Prices scale the totals but do not change the split.

    4.1 Three things this comparison cannot settle on its own

    • Most of the page is a sensitivity test, not a measurement. The stress-year yields are constructed from the loss share and the buffering, which builds in "same share lost". The field-trial test in 2.2 is the first real evidence, and it is split and weak.
    • CGIAR's draft indicator is "same tonnes lost" by definition. It measures effects in kilograms per hectare. Reading it as a share is a change request to that team, not a clarification.
    • The Atlas and the indicator count stress seasons differently. The Atlas's "moderate or worse" band fires in half to four-fifths of seasons; the indicator's benchmarks aim for about one in five. A factor built against one cannot be multiplied by the other.

    5 · Recommendation for the CCA analysis

    1. Use "same share lost" (F3), on first principles rather than on the trial evidence. It is the rule that never calls a protective practice maladaptive and that matches how crop-loss evidence is usually expressed (as shares). The trial test in 2.2 does not decide between share and tonnes; it is split and underpowered. Keep buffering signed, so a practice that worsens bad years shows as negative; do not floor at zero.
    2. Fix the current pipeline. Shrink the normal-year gain in stress years; compute avoided loss on the practice's yield rather than the control's; remove the clamp that forces buffering into 0–1; use normal-season yield gains once they exist per practice.
    3. Treat the growing-season definition as part of the factor. Record the season window with every factor, and give the Atlas hazard tiers crop- and region-specific seasons before using them as stress frequencies.
    4. Do not multiply trial-based factors by the Atlas hazard frequency without reconciling the two. In Kenya the Atlas counts three to four times as many stress seasons as the weather records. Use an observed-climate frequency (the hazards team's planned observed baseline), or state which benchmark a factor was estimated against and apply that one.
    5. Keep the three severity bands and hand the indicator one factor per hazard by the exact merge. Report hazards separately until co-occurring hazards are handled.
    6. Scope: maize, cover crops and reduced tillage, three countries. Other crops and practices follow once their loss shares have evidence behind them.

    6 · Questions for the Adaptation Insights team

    1. When you estimate a practice's effect in stress and normal seasons, will it be a ratio or kilograms per hectare? If kilograms, which yield converts it? (The indicator's reference database is a design, not yet a dataset, so this is still open.)
    2. Can the control's stress-season loss be recorded alongside each factor? Without it the two readings cannot be converted into each other.
    3. Which stress benchmark will you use, and would factors kept by severity band, merged exactly into one per hazard, be acceptable?
    4. Shared task: classify the ERA maize trial seasons as stress or normal, so normal- and stress-season effects can be estimated for the practices in scope. The CGIAR climate-toolkit already does this from rainfall and temperature records; this page used it.
